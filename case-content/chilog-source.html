ChiLog

A pet health tracking app concept built for speed and clarity

Designed the end-to-end UX and UI for a mobile app concept that helps pet owners log daily health activities in seconds, spot patterns across symptoms and behaviors over time, and walk into vet visits with a clear health summary — built around the insight that most pet tracking tools are either too slow to use daily or too scattered to be useful when it matters.

MY ROLE

Product Designer (solo project)

TOOLS

Figma, Claude Design

Timeline

May 2026 - Present

01

Problem

Problem Statement

Pet owners struggle to consistently track their pet's health (especially for overall healthy dogs) due to high logging friction, scattered information, and lack of sync between caregivers, making it impossible to recall symptoms clearly at vet visits or prevent mistakes like accidental double-dosing.

Pain Points

Logging friction

Most tracking tools require too many steps to record a single entry. The effort outweighs the habit, so owners skip logging altogether, especially for quick observations like bathroom frequency or unusual behavior.

Scattered & unrecallable information

Health data ends up spread across journal apps, chat messages, and memory. At the vet, owners can't answer basic questions — "When did the soft stool start?" or "What food brand is she on?" — because there's no single organized place to look.

Multi-caregiver blind spots

When two people share pet care with no shared system, things fall through the cracks. A medication gets given twice because neither person knew the other already did it.

Competitive Analysis

Three gaps no competitor solved:

  • No app combines quick logging + straightforward info storage + record history

  • No app visualizes health data as a cross-category chart to let you see how symptoms, bathroom habits, and meals relate to each other for vet visits

  • Vet visit summary is either absent or paywalled across every app tested

03

Define

Jobs to be Done

To ground design decisions in real user needs, I developed two JBTD based on survey responses and personal experience.

JTBD 1 — The Daily Logger

Based on Maya, freelance designer, 1 dog, shared caregiver

When something happens with my dog — a soft stool, a skipped meal, an unusual behavior — I want to record it instantly without stopping what I'm doing, so I can build an accurate picture of her health over time without relying on memory.

  • Pain: Current tools require too many steps.

  • Trigger: Noticing something worth logging in the middle of daily life.

  • Success: Logged in under 5 seconds, no typing required.

JTBD 2 — The Vet Preparer

Based on Shelly, full-time employee, 2 cats, shared caregiver

When I'm about to go to the vet, I want a clear summary of what's happened since the last visit, so I can answer the vet's questions accurately and not waste the appointment trying to remember details.

  • Pain: Information is scattered across chat messages, memory, and multiple apps.

  • Trigger: Vet appointment coming up, realizing she can't recall recent symptoms.

  • Success: A one-tap summary she can pull up or screenshot in the waiting room.

How Might We

From the research & JTBD analysis, I reframed the pain points as design opportunities:

HMW make logging so fast that owners do it in the moment, not "later"?

HMW help caregivers know instantly whether medication has already been given?

HMW surface health patterns automatically without the owner having to dig through logs?

HMW give owners a clear health snapshot before a vet visit — in one tap?

Key Screen Decisions - Health Timeline

Old Design

Final Design

The biggest design challenge was making health data readable without overwhelming the user.

Separate charts per category looked clean but made it impossible to spot correlations. You couldn’t see that the day your pet had diarrhea was also the day she barely ate.

The timeline view solves this by aligning all categories on the same date axis. Each category uses a different visual language suited to its data type:

  • Dot count for meal and poo

  • Number badge for pee frequency

  • Large dot with count badge for symptoms

07

Evaluation

What I learned

Less is more.

The hardest design decisions weren’t about adding features, they were about knowing what to leave out. Deciding that diarrhea belongs in Symptoms rather than Poo Condition, or that “Flag this log” is more honest than auto-creating a symptom entry, required understanding the user’s mental model to make the call with confidence.

Data visualization is only useful if it changes behavior.

Early in the process I assumed charts were the answer to making health data useful. It took mapping the actual vet visit scenario to realize that separate charts per category don’t actually help owners spot what matters. The Health Timeline came out of asking: what would actually help Maya answer her vet’s questions? — not what chart type looks good here?

Design decisions compound.

Every structural choice: separating Log from Schedule, making pet context global, and minimum mandatory fields, had downstream effects on every screen that followed. Getting the IA right early made the rest of the design faster and more consistent.

What I’d do differently

If I had more time, I would have conducted usability testing with real pet owners before finalizing the high-fidelity screens. Several decisions like the Flag this log checkbox and the timeline dot system were validated through logic and personal experience, but would benefit from watching someone use them for the first time.

I’d also revisit the Vet Summary structure with actual vets to understand what information they find most useful during a consultation. The current design is built entirely from the owner’s perspective.
